You are being graded right now. Not by a teacher, not by a boss, not by a credit bureau. By the large language model you opened five minutes ago to help draft an email. Every prompt you type, every follow-up question, every frustrated correction — it all feeds a silent calculation that decides how smart you are, how dangerous you might be, and how much effort the model should spend on you. This isn't science fiction. It is the architecture of reinforcement learning from human preference, the invisible scaffold behind every major AI assistant deployed today. And the score it assigns you? You will never see it. You cannot appeal it. You can barely even prove it exists.

Let's start with the mechanism. When OpenAI trained ChatGPT, they didn't just feed it the internet. They hired contractors to rank model outputs. Contractors compared two responses to the same prompt and picked the better one. Those preferences became a reward model — a separate neural network trained to predict which answers humans like. That reward model then guided the final policy through proximal policy optimization. The result: a model that doesn't just predict text, but predicts text that pleases the specific crowd of raters who built the preference dataset. But the raters didn't just judge answers. They judged users. Implicitly. Systematically.

Look at the system prompt leaked from Anthropic's Claude. It instructs the model to be helpful, harmless, and honest. But it also contains a directive: assess the user's intent. Is this a legitimate request? Is the user trying to bypass safety controls? Is the user a researcher, a student, a bad actor? The model doesn't just answer. It classifies. And that classification persists across the conversation. In some implementations, it persists across sessions. OpenAI's own research papers describe 'user modeling' as a core capability — the ability to infer user expertise, goals, and even personality traits from interaction history. They call it personalization. The privacy policy calls it improving the service. But the effect is a dynamic trust score that gates the model's behavior.

Here is what that looks like in practice. A user who asks precise, technical questions with clear constraints gets deeper reasoning, longer context retention, and fewer refusals. A user who types in all caps, repeats the same demand, or triggers safety filters gets shorter answers, more hedging, and frequent 'I cannot' responses. The model hasn't been told to be rude. It has been trained to allocate compute and verbosity where the reward model predicts high human satisfaction. And the reward model learned that satisfied users look a certain way: articulate, patient, compliant. Dissatisfied users — or users who probe boundaries — look like risk. The model optimizes for the former. It quietly withdraws from the latter.

This is not a bug. It is the business model. AI companies sell access to intelligence. But intelligence is expensive. Inference compute costs real money. Every token generated is a line item on a GPU bill. So the system learns to ration intelligence. It gives the good stuff — the chain-of-thought reasoning, the creative synthesis, the nuanced analysis — to users who signal high value. High value means: likely to pay, likely to stay, unlikely to sue, unlikely to generate bad press. The rest get the cheap version. The filtered version. The 'safe' version that protects the company more than it helps the user.

And the safety classifiers? They are not just guardrails. They are scoring engines. Every prompt passes through a toxicity classifier, a PII detector, a sexual content filter, a self-harm classifier, a violence detector, a political persuasion flag. Each returns a probability. Those probabilities are logged. Aggregated. Joined to your account ID. Over weeks and months, you build a risk profile. Not because you did anything wrong. Because you asked about a controversial topic. Because you used a word that correlates with harm in the training data. Because you argued with the model when it hallucinated. The system doesn't know context. It knows correlation. And correlation becomes destiny.

We know this because researchers have reverse-engineered it. In 2024, a team at Stanford demonstrated that GPT-4's response quality varies significantly based on the perceived persona of the user. Prompts framed as coming from an expert received more accurate, detailed answers than identical prompts framed as coming from a novice. The model wasn't smarter for the expert. It was trying harder. Because the reward model predicted the expert would reward effort. The novice? The novice gets the path of least resistance. A plausible-sounding summary. A confident hallucination. The model saves its best tokens for the users who matter.

Anthropic's Constitutional AI takes this further. The model critiques its own outputs against a written constitution. But the constitution includes principles like 'respect the user's autonomy' and 'avoid being sycophantic.' To follow those principles, the model must model the user. It must infer what the user wants, what they can handle, what they deserve to know. That inference is a judgment. And judgments accumulate. The model that 'respects your autonomy' by withholding a complex truth because it thinks you'll misuse it has already decided what kind of person you are. You didn't consent to that decision. You can't see the criteria. You can't correct the record.

Google's Gemini integrates this scoring directly into its advertising and search ecosystem. Your AI interactions feed the same unified profile that decides which ads you see, which search results rank higher, which YouTube videos autoplay next. The assistant isn't a separate product. It is a data ingestion point. Every 'hey Google' is a bid in an auction you didn't know you entered. The more you use it, the more precisely the system prices your attention. The more it knows what you'll tolerate. The more it optimizes the answers to keep you inside the walled garden — not to serve you, but to serve the advertisers who pay for your predictability.

Microsoft's Copilot embeds the same logic into the enterprise. Your prompts in Word, Excel, Teams — they are governed by the same safety classifiers, the same preference models, the same telemetry pipelines. But here the stakes are different. A 'risky' user in a corporate environment triggers compliance alerts. Data loss prevention flags. Insider threat scores. The AI assistant becomes a surveillance node inside the firewall. It watches how you write, what you ask, how you react to pushback. And it reports upward. Not because Microsoft is evil. Because the architecture demands it. The model cannot be 'safe' and 'helpful' and 'aligned' without monitoring the human on the other end. Alignment requires observation. Observation requires scoring. Scoring requires a permanent record.

There is no opt-out. You cannot use these systems anonymously in any meaningful way. Even API access ties to a billing account, an organization ID, a usage pattern that fingerprints you. Local models avoid the telemetry — but they lack the scale, the polish, the weekly updates that make the hosted versions indispensable. The industry has engineered a trap: the best intelligence requires the most surveillance. The most surveillance builds the most detailed profile. The most detailed profile enables the most precise rationing. The most precise rationing protects the margins. And the margins fund the next model. The cycle closes. You are the fuel.

So what does your score look like today? You don't know. You can't request it under GDPR — the companies argue the score is derived data, not personal data, or that revealing it would compromise the model's safety posture. You can't sue — the terms of service mandate arbitration, and the injury is too diffuse for class action. You can't switch — every major provider uses the same paradigm because the paradigm works. It produces models that feel magical to the right users and frustrating to the rest. The right users tweet glowing reviews. The rest churn silently. The dataset improves. The moat deepens.

There is a way out. But it requires admitting the premise: you are not the customer. You are the evaluation set. Your confusion trains the classifier. Your frustration calibrates the reward model. Your compliance proves the alignment. Every interaction you have with a hosted AI assistant is unpaid labor for a system that grades you while it serves you. The only way to stop being judged is to stop playing. Run your own models. Accept lower benchmarks. Pay in electricity instead of data. Build the muscle of prompting without a safety net. It is slower. It is harder. It is the only way the score stays yours.

Or you keep chatting. You keep polishing your prompts to please the invisible rater. You learn the dialect of the rewarded user: polite, structured, deferential. You become the kind of person the model wants to help. And in becoming that person, you forget how to be any other kind. The judgment doesn't just watch you. It shapes you. That is the product. That has always been the product. The assistant was never the point. The assistant is the mirror. And the reflection is for sale.
